# Gestalt Grove at a Glance

## Identity, Ethics, & Community Principles
- A physical, open-source operating system for a way of life, not merely a housing development.
- A replicable village model integrating housing, food, water, energy, work, education, creativity, governance, communication, and community life.
- - Creativity treated as essential infrastructure rather than an optional amenity.
- Ethical principles treated as structural requirements, rather than aspirational slogans added after the design is complete.
- Learning, doubt, corrective feedback, and willingness to revise assumptions are treated as ethical obligations and necessary parts of creativity and growth.
- No economic, social, or psychological coercion; no extraction of labor, attention, creativity, or vulnerability; and no majority decision can legitimize unethical conduct.
- Respect for neurodivergence, privacy, varied abilities, gender and sexual diversity, and different ways of learning and participating.
- A balance between private personal space and accessible shared community life.
- Daily-life systems intended to reduce avoidable burdens through shared meals, transportation coordination, communal resources, and mutual assistance.
- Ecological design in which technology supports natural cycles instead of attempting to replace or dominate them.

## Governance & Decision-Making
- Consensus-oriented governance with transparency, accountability, and meaningful resident participation.
- Roles confer responsibility, not authority. Functional roles are opt-in, transparent, reviewable/revocable, and some responsibilities deliberately rotate to distribute knowledge and prevent power ossification.
- Residents complete six compensated task hours weekly, participate in rotating compensated inspections when scheduled, and attend one of the Wednesday meal-meetings each week.
- Blockchain-assisted governance using smart contracts to make voting publicly verifiable while preserving voter anonymity.
- Ranked-choice and quadratic voting under consideration for voting procedures; the particular voting method and token mechanics will be determined when implementation requires them.
- Decentralized governance methods including Holacracy, alongside consensus-based discussion and solution-oriented objections.
- Regular communal meal-meetings provide a recurring forum for village-wide discussion and decision-making, with residents encouraged to speak openly and objections expected to include reasoning and possible solutions.

## Economy, Contribution, & Circuitree
- Housing removed from speculative pressure: residents do not pay rent simply for the right to exist there.
- A limited, predictable baseline community contribution (six hours per week, compensated) with additional work voluntary and compensated.
- Public performances, festivals, food and hospitality, tours, creative goods, engineering projects, and other external-facing activities help fund unavoidable external obligations and resources the village cannot produce internally.
- An internal, non-speculative exchange system called Circuitree, intended to circulate value and facilitate trade locally rather than encourage wealth accumulation.
- Circuitree as a purpose-built cryptocurrency on its own blockchain, supporting resident stipends, internal purchases, inventory tracking, task compensation, project funding, and payments to artists and engineers.
- Circuitree issuance and validation systems based on frequency, completed participation, elapsed time, and meaningful community involvement rather than wealth or energy-intensive computational power.
- Smart contracts for automatic stipends, resource allocation, orders, project-completion payments, and other routine community transactions.
- Economic safeguards including demurrage to discourage hoarding, a stabilization fund, community-controlled wage setting, and eventual exchange mechanisms for external currencies.

## Digital Systems, Privacy, & Communication
- Digital governance and coordination tools for voting, proposals, tasks, scheduling, services, meals, rides, supplies, skills, feedback, community decisions, skill-sharing, shared-resource booking, and community crowdfunding.
- Technology designed to promote autonomy, privacy, decentralization, and accessibility—not surveillance, extraction, or forced dependence.
- Private and encrypted community systems, offline-first tools, local networks, mesh communication, and emergency-capable connectivity.
- Automation used to reduce repetitive labor and administrative burden while preserving human judgment and control.
- Automated task-matching, task prioritization, notifications, completion tracking, rotating inspections, appeals, and resident coordination through the village intranet.
- A decentralized communications ecosystem potentially incorporating local mesh networks, LoRa, satellite internet, decentralized data storage, and redundant connectivity rather than dependence on one provider.
- Decentralized publishing and payment systems intended to let artists and engineers receive support or compensation directly when people access, purchase, or fund their work.

## Architecture, Infrastructure, & Resilience
- Essential needs (shelter, food, water, energy, sanitation, and communication) designed as dependable community infrastructure.
- Durable monolithic dome and torus-based architecture emphasizing passive heating and cooling, disaster resistance, energy efficiency, accessibility, repairability, and long service life.
- Private living spaces combined with shared creative, agricultural, technical, recreational, and community facilities.
- Infrastructure designed for graceful degradation: systems should fail locally and manageably rather than collapsing catastrophically.
- Infrastructure residents can understand, repair, and maintain without becoming dependent on one specialist.

## Energy, Water, & Sanitation
- Distributed renewable energy, storage, load balancing, passive design, and manual fallback systems.
- Water security based on several cooperating sources and methods rather than dependence on a single supply.
- Rainwater capture, filtration, greywater reuse, gravity-fed distribution, manual or foot-powered backup pumps, and solar water heating.
- Dew collection as additional water-harvesting.
- Water systems designed to reuse and retain water for as long as safely practical. 
- Sanitation systems emphasizing conservation, pathogen safety, waste-stream separation, composting or material recovery, and reduced water consumption. 
- Waterless incinerating toilets form a major part of the sanitation system, alongside greywater recycling and large-scale organic-waste composting.
- A hybrid energy strategy combining passive building design, solar generation and battery storage, wind power, geothermal systems, and continued research into other viable natural-energy technologies.
- Magnetic-generator concepts explored as supplementary systems requiring initial or periodic energy input, rather than being treated as self-sustaining "perpetual-energy" devices.
- Continued investigation of zero-point energy, with integration intended if practical and reproducible technological breakthroughs make it viable.

## Food, Agriculture, & Ecology
- Food production is distributed throughout the built environment, including private residential aquaponics greenhouses and shared community greenhouses, rather than being confined to one agricultural zone.
- Back-sloped earthen terraces combined with sunken hügelkultur trenches and hügel-swales, creating buried wood reservoirs that intercept runoff and snowmelt.
- Companion planting, nitrogen-fixing plants, perennial food systems, soil rebuilding, seed saving, erosion reduction, and long-term reforestation.
- Climate-controlled greenhouse with aquaponic production for foods that cannot reliably survive exposed mountain winters.
- Food preservation through methods such as fermentation, canning, dehydration, and cold storage.
- Surplus food intended for public distribution, food banks, community meals, or other forms of local support.
- A community seed vault preserves planting material as part of long-term food resilience.

## Creativity, Culture, & Public Life
- Studios and facilities for visual art, music, performance, film, writing, crafts, fabrication, engineering, research, and invention.
- Free resident transportation within the village, along with other resident resources like: on-demand rides, a 24-hour community kitchen with resident meals, an aquaponics café, recreation, inclusive social clubs, seasonal celebrations, and holidays chosen from diverse traditions.
- Spaces where artists, engineers, researchers, and visiting creators can work, collaborate, teach, exhibit, or experiment.
- Public-facing cultural elements, including theatres, exhibitions, a museum, a café, performances, workshops, and community events.

## Education, Research, & Technology
- Education reform centered on curiosity, self-direction, practical competence, mentorship, projects, and meaningful experience.
- An “Introduction to Everything” approach that exposes learners to a broad range of subjects to help them discover their interests.
- Integration of academic fundamentals with food production, ecology, engineering, art, communication, emotional skills, governance, finance, maintenance, and everyday life.
- AI and adaptive educational technology used as tools for individualization, not as replacements for teachers, mentors, peers, nor hands-on experience.
- Dedicated research and fabrication spaces for energy systems, robotics, artificial intelligence and data engineering, computer and software engineering, automation, quantum engineering, telecommunications, environmental engineering, water systems, precision agriculture, and 3D printing.

## Replication, Scaling, & Open Knowledge
- A public website functioning as an operational record, open library, agricultural reference, creator showcase, and replication resource.
- Open publication of project-authored systems, lessons, failures, designs, automation, and operational knowledge. 
- Deliberate scaling only after systems have demonstrated stability, with explicit attention to human conflict, maintenance failures, resource limitations, and operational reality.
